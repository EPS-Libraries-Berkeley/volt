## I. Overview of databases and other search tools
Introduction
Before searching for relevant, reliable information, there is a choice to be made: Should you use an academic database? What about UC Library Search, another search engine, or AI? There are many differences between these approaches, including what sources they pull from, and how much control you have over the search process.

An academic database is recommended for research because it is an organized, often subject-specific collection of published scholarly materials which supports efficient, reproducible searching. At UC Berkeley, the Library provides access to over 1000 different databases. These databases provide robust search, filter and sort features. They contain searchable citations of peer-reviewed articles from thousands of different journals, as well as other sources such as book chapters and conference proceedings. They provide all the information you need to evaluate a source for credibility, such as author name, publication details, and a summary. 

A database platform is the software interface used to search and access one or multiple databases at once. We might become familiar with common platform names, such as EBSCO, Elsevier, and ProQuest, simply because they are posted prominently above database titles and article titles. UC Library Search searches across UC Berkeley Library holdings, other UC campus libraries, the California Digital Library (CDL), and more. Use the filters for “resource type” or “peer-reviewed journals” to find a variety of materials, such as books, media, archival collections, and many journal articles. However, UC Library search is not as in-depth as a subject-specific database search. 

A search engine, such as Google, Yahoo!, or DuckDuckGo, uses computer algorithms to search the Internet. Search results are not reproducible, likely varying between users, searches, and devices, and information found on the open web must be critically evaluated. With thorough evaluation of sources for relevance and reliability, a search engine may be useful to find government webpages and other openly accessible content which is not behind publisher paywalls. While Google Scholar limits its search of the Internet to scholarly resource types, such as journals, conference papers, theses and dissertations, academic books, pre-prints, abstracts, and technical reports, it lacks the accuracy and sorting features that databases provide, and is more likely to include unreliable sources. Because Google Scholar searches the Internet and not all its sources are peer-reviewed, results must be critically evaluated.

A generative AI tool may seem like a quick way to find some information online. However, when you need original sources of relevant, reliable information, tracing and critically evaluating every source is actually time-consuming. If choosing to use one of these tools, always follow course and campus AI policies. It is important to understand how these tools work and how they are limited. 

- Potential bias: AI tools may reflect biases or errors in their training process or source material (particularly if they are trained on or searching the open web).
- Ineffective prompts: AI tools generally provide better answers when prompts are more specific and precise. At the beginning of a research project it can be difficult to formulate precise queries because you are just beginning to learn about a domain.
- Generality: AI tools may provide better answers when the LLM has specifically been trained or draws on a relevant corpus (as in Retrieval-Augmented Generation, or RAG). ChatGPT, Gemini, and many other general generative AI tools are trained on text scraped from the open web, which is not domain-specific and includes misleading, biased, false, and harmful information.
- Not reproducible: Search results will vary between users, searches, and devices.
