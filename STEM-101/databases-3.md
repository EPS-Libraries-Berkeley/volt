### Other search tools

What about UC Library Search, Google Scholar, or generative AI? There are many differences between these approaches, including what sources they pull from and how much control you have over the search process.

:::{hint} When to use the library catalog: UC Library Search 
:class: dropdown

Use **UC Library Search (UCLS)** to find a variety of materials (books, media, archival collections, and many journal articles), or to look up a specific title. Here, you may search across UC Berkeley Library holdings, other UC campus libraries, the California Digital Library (CDL), and more. You may also filter by “resource type” or “peer-reviewed journals”. However, UC Library Search is not as in-depth as a subject-specific database search. 
:::

:::{hint} What to know about search engines & generative AI
:class: dropdown

A **search engine**, such as Google, Yahoo!, or DuckDuckGo, uses computer algorithms to search the Internet. Search results are not reproducible, likely varying between users, searches, and devices, and information found on the open web must be critically evaluated. With thorough evaluation of sources for relevance and reliability, a search engine may be useful to find government webpages and other openly accessible content which is not behind publisher paywalls. While **Google Scholar** limits its search of the Internet to scholarly resource types, such as journals, conference papers, theses and dissertations, academic books, pre-prints, abstracts, and technical reports, it lacks the accuracy and sorting features that databases provide, and is more likely to include unreliable sources.

A **generative AI** tool may seem like a quick way to find some information online. However, when you need original sources of relevant and reliable information, tracing and critically evaluating every source is actually time-consuming. If choosing to use one of these tools, always follow course and campus AI policies. It is important to understand how these tools work and how they are limited. 

**Issues to Consider:**

- Potential bias: AI tools may reflect biases or errors in their training process or source material (particularly if they are trained on or searching the open web).
- Ineffective prompts: AI tools generally provide better answers when prompts are more specific and precise. At the beginning of a research project it can be difficult to formulate precise queries because you are just beginning to learn about a domain.
- Generality: AI tools may provide better answers when the LLM has specifically been trained or draws on a relevant corpus (as in Retrieval-Augmented Generation, or RAG). ChatGPT, Gemini, and many other general generative AI tools are trained on text scraped from the open web, which is not domain-specific and includes misleading, biased, false, and harmful information.
- Not reproducible: Search results will vary between users, searches, and devices. 
:::

## A-Z Databases for finding and selecting databases

### Introduction
A-Z Databases is a descriptive list of databases and other resources that will help you find the most relevant and reliable information sources for your research. It’s the best way to find databases because you can search categories and descriptions.

### Access
A-Z Databases can be accessed from the UC Library Search Databases tab.

### Finding Databases
Databases can be general (covering many subjects) or subject-specific.
- **General databases (examples):** 
  - Academic Search Complete
  - Scopus
  - Web of Science
  
- **Subject-specific databases (examples):**
  - PubMed: medicine, molecular biology, public health
  - SciFinder: biochemistry, chemical engineering, chemistry
  - GeoRef: geology, geosciences

### Searching A-Z Databases
- database **title** (example: PubMed) or descriptive **keyword** (example: standards)
- **subjects** (example: bioengineering)
- **types of sources** (example: videos)
- vendors (not needed for most searches)

```{image} ./images/A-to-Z-1
:class: border border-secondary 
:alt: A-Z Databases Interface
:width: 800px
:align: center
```

**Title searching:** Be sure you have the exact title, including spacing. “Geoscience World” returns no results; only “GeoScienceWorld” retrieves the link to the database of geological literature.

**Keyword searching:** Remember that you are trying to use words that match database descriptions, so be careful of using terms that are too specific to a particular topic. Searching on “breast cancer” retrieves no results, but searching on “health” retrieves over 200 information sources, including PubMed. If you’re looking for subjects (for example, biophysics) or resource types (for example, standards) that don’t appear in the drop-down menus, try a keyword search. Results are ordered by A-Z Databases’ relevance ranking, which may or may not  match your own determination of relevance.

:::{note} Pro tip!
Boolean operators are not supported in keyword searching. Searching on “biomedicine” returns three resources, and searching on “medicine” returns 50 resources. But searching on (biomedicine OR medicine) returns no results.
:::

**Subject searching:** Under the “Subjects” drop-down menu, you can select one or more subjects relevant to your research topic. Librarian-recommended information sources will be in the “Recommended!” box at the top of the results list. Both the resources in the “Recommended!” box and the results list will be ordered alphabetically.

**Combining categories:** Different search categories can be combined (example: Subjects: Public Health and Types: Statistics and Numeric Data), and the results will be listed alphabetically.

```{image} ./images/A-to-Z-2
:class: border border-secondary 
:alt: A-Z Databases Interface with "Statistics and Numeric Data" selected from the "sources" dropdown list
:width: 800px

```

**Multiple entries** in each search category can be selected (example: Types:Images, Videos). Multiple choices are searched with an implied OR: resources in any of the selected categories will be retrieved.


:::{note} Searching tips!
- You must click the Search button to apply the categories you’ve selected for your search.
- Selections are persistent, so be sure to uncheck previous entries when beginning a new search if they are no longer relevant.
:::

Searches may not be comprehensive, depending on how the search terms match with the way the databases have been described and categorized (example: many of the resources in the “medicine” category would likely also be relevant to the “biomedicine” category).

## Tracing Citations

If an article is relevant to your topic, you will want to look at the research it is built on and has cited (backward citation). But it can often be very helpful to see more recent work that has cited the article (forward citation). There are several different ways to do this, and the results will overlap -- no single method is comprehensive.

```{image} ./images/Citation-tracing
:class: border border-secondary 
:alt: Graphic showing how 3 sample articles are interconnected through backward and forward citation.
:width: 800px
:align: center
```

[Google Scholar](http://scholar.google.com/) provides forward citations for most sources. It has a broader range of documents included (not just peer-reviewed journals, but reports, pre-prints, etc.) and doesn't eliminate self citation or de-duplicate the results.

[Web of Science](https://libproxy.berkeley.edu/login?qurl=http://webofknowledge.com/WOS) is a vast, multidisciplinary database, which includes the Science and the Social Science Citation Indexes. This index allows you to do a "Cited Reference" search if you select that tab. This will retrieve other articles (from a prestigious list of peer-reviewed journals) which have cited the target article, and it also shows the references for the original article... both forward and backward citation.

A. **medRxiv**: A preprint service for medical, clinical, and health sciences. Preprints "should not be relied on to guide clinical practice or health-related behavior and should not be reported in news media as established information." 

B. **PubMed**: Citations and abstracts [from journals] in the fields of biomedicine and health, as well as portions of the life sciences, behavioral sciences, chemical sciences, and bioengineering.

**Correct answer: B**
Why B is correct: Biomedicine and health are subject areas relevant to the topic of the health effects of wildfire smoke, and scientific journals in the fields of biomedicine and health indexed in PubMed are peer-reviewed.
Why A is incorrect: Although the subject area, medical and health sciences, is relevant, preprints are articles posted before undergoing peer review.
