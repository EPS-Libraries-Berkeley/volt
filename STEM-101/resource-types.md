When doing research in STEM fields, you will come across many different types of publications and resources. You may have already seen some of the more common types - scholarly journal articles, conference papers, books - but there are also some uncommon ones that can be useful in your research. 

Publications can be written for distinct audiences and provide different types of information. Becoming familiar with publication types and when they are produced during the research lifecycle can help you find the information you need faster.
## Timeline of a development of a topic

Scientific publication types often align with an initial event, discovery or idea proposal. Publication proceeds on different timelines with some initial findings being presented over weeks or months, and longer term studies or analysis occurring over years. Ultimately this is also a **cyclical** process with idea, data and findings reimagined as new information becomes available.

## Primary, secondary, and tertiary sources in STEM

The scientific literature is categorized into three main tiers depending on how close the material is to the original research event.

::::{grid} 1 2 3 3
:gutter: 3

:::{card} 
:header: **Primary Sources**
_First-Hand Research_
***
Presents or comments on direct, first-hand research, field data, or laboratory experiments. Highly specialized and current.
:::

:::{card} 
:header: **Secondary Sources**
_Synthesis & Overview_
***
Summarizes and synthesizes primary findings. Ideal for gaining a high-level overview of a research domain and locating key citations.
:::

:::{card} 
:header: **Tertiary Sources**
_Foundational Facts_
***
Compiles broad, discipline-level background information and well-established scientific facts. Not typically referenced heavily.
:::
::::

:::{tab-item} Secondary Sources
**Focus:** Synthesis, critique, & practical applications

| Format | Definition | Key Characteristics | Limitations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Literature Reviews** | Critical evaluation and synthesis of multiple primary papers | Identifies research gaps; offers exhaustive reference lists | Inherently lags behind the newest primary publications |

**Focus:** Broad, foundational background information

| Format | Definition | Key Characteristics | Limitations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Encyclopedias** | Broad topic summaries written by domain experts | Great entry point; includes introductory bibliographies | Can quickly become outdated |
| **Textbooks** | Comprehensive overviews of core discipline concepts | Pedagogy-focused; structured for sequential learning | Ignores recent fringe research or ongoing debates |
| **Handbooks** | Reference books designed for quick fact-checking | Quick access to physical constants, formulas, and rules | Lacks deep theoretical explanations |

## Emerging and Ephemeral Formats

**Grey Literature** refers to sources produced by organizations where publishing is not the primary activity. Primary sources mentioned above like dissertations, technical reports, and government white papers are grey literature, as are some secondary and tertiary resources. While they often bypass standard peer review, they retain high scientific value.

Modern research, however, increasingly involves **ephemeral digital outputs**:

::::{grid} 1 2 2 2

:::{grid-item-card} 🏛️ Grey Literature
* **Issuer:** Stable bodies (e.g., NASA, WHO, universities)
* **Storage:** Archival institutional repositories
* **Metadata:** Standard DOIs, dates, report numbers
* **Characteristic:** Static, finalized documents
:::

:::{grid-item-card} 💬 Ephemeral Outputs
* **Issuer:** Individual creators (Substack, Reddit, X/Twitter)
* **Storage:** Self-published or hosted on third-party platforms
* **Metadata:** Inconsistent or missing indexing; high link rot
* **Characteristic:** Dynamic, conversational, iteratively edited
:::
::::

:::{hint} How to Handle Ephemeral Outputs in a Research Paper
:class: dropdown

| Category | Examples | Characteristics | Citation Guidance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Presentations** | Webinars, Lecture Slides | High-value knowledge transfer; dependent on live context | Citable if archived in permanent repositories (e.g., Zenodo) with a DOI |
| **Newsletters/Blogs** | Substack, Medium | Fast dissemination of expert commentary with high-quality expert commentary; no institutional review | Cite with caution; high risk of post-publication edits |
| **Community Threads \& Conversations** | Reddit, X/Twitter | Real-time collaborative discussion and field sentiment | Cite for qualitative social trends, not factual scientific claims |
| **Machine Artifacts** | AI Prompts & LLM Outputs | Non-reproducible outputs across runs | Append full prompt and output transcript per style guides (APA/MLA) |
:::

## Evaluating Resources
Before using any sources in your work, you should be prepared to evaluate its relevancy for your information needs. Consider the following questions for any source:

- **Authority** - Who is the author? What is their point of view? 
- **Purpose** - Why was the source created? Who is the intended audience?
- **Publication & format** - Where was it published? In what medium?
- **Relevance** - How is it relevant to your research? What is its scope?
- **Date of publication** - When was it written? Has it been updated?
- **Documentation** - Did they cite their sources? Who did they cite?

For a deeper dive on source evaluation, see our [Evaluating Resources Guide](https://guides.lib.berkeley.edu/evaluating-resources).

::::{tip} Evaluation in action: let’s review a Wikipedia article using these basic standards.
You are working in a wildfire research laboratory and are looking for information on the history of the development of wildland-urban interface zones to support a literature review. Let’s look at [this Wikipedia article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wildland%E2%80%93urban_interface#) and use our evaluation questions to assess whether this resource would be appropriate to use as a citation in your paper. 

```{image} ./images/wikipedia-wui.png
:alt: brief view of first two paragraphs of the wikipedia entry on wildland urban interface
:width: 500px
:align: center
```

:::{tip .simple .dropdown icon=false open=false} See Evaluation Breakdown

* ❌ **Authorship:** Openly and anonymously edited. Expertise cannot be verified.
* ❌ **Purpose:** Intended for a general audience.
* ❌ **Publication & format:** Wikipedia is a non-profit that explicitly does not vouch for content accuracy.
* ⚠️ **Relevance:** Mixes well-supported facts with unsourced claims.
* ✅ **Date of publication:** Actively maintained with recent edit timestamps.
* ✅ **Documentation:** Includes an extensive list of external primary citations.

**Verdict:** **Do not cite directly.** While useful for preliminary orientation and finding primary citations at the bottom of the page, Wikipedia fails the authority requirement. (See [Wikipedia's guide on academic use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Academic_use)).
:::

:::::{attention} Knowledge Check 2: Audiences 
:class: dropdown open
**Scenario:** You have developed a novel machine-learning algorithm that improves early wildfire detection by 35%. You need to communicate findings to three distinct groups. Match the audience with the most appropriate publication type.

::::{grid} 1 2 2 2

:::{grid-item-card} Audience
1. **Computer science researchers** who want to reproduce the model structure.
2. **Emergency management directors** who need actionable deployment advice.
3. The **general public** interested in local wildfire risk.
:::

:::{grid-item-card} Publication Type
A. Policy brief  
B. Podcast interview  
C. Peer-Reviewed Journal Article  
:::
::::

:::{attention .simple .dropdown icon=false open=false} Check Answer
**Correct Answer: 1C, 2A, 3B**

**Explanation:** 
- **Computer Science Researchers:** Require the full methodological depth of a **Peer-Reviewed Journal Article** for replication.
- **Emergency Directors:** Require concise, actionable **Policy Briefs** for deployment.
- **General Public:** Benefit from accessible audio media like a **Podcast Interview**.
:::

:class: dropdown open
Which criteria would you **not** use when evaluating a source for relevancy?

A. Authority  
B. Length  
C. Purpose

E. Documentation  

:::{attention .simple .dropdown icon=false open=false} Check Answer
**Correct Answer: B**

**Explanation:**   
**Authority, purpose, date of publication, and documentation** should be considered, along with **publication & format**, when evaluating a source for relevancy. The **length** of the source does not provide any helpful indication of relevancy.
